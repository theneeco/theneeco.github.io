TPRM Assessment Tracker

Nikhil Kubsad  ·  November 2026

A deterministic tool that runs the whole vendor risk reassessment lifecycle: request, chase, receive, score, and a human decision that resets the clock. Built to replace a manual, spreadsheet-driven TPRM process that only works as well as whoever remembers to update it.

Built with: Python standard library only (python-dotenv optional) · SMTP email · SHA-256 hash-chained audit log

Frameworks mapped: ISO/IEC 27001:2022 A.5.19-A.5.22, supplier relationships and information security in the supply chain

The problem

Most third-party risk programmes run on a spreadsheet and a calendar reminder. Someone has to remember which vendor is due for reassessment, chase the ones who go quiet, manually mark up a returned questionnaire against a framework, and keep enough of a trail to prove any of it happened if an auditor asks. That works for a handful of vendors. It stops working somewhere past that, and the audit trail is only as reliable as whoever remembered to update the spreadsheet.

The fix isn't a smarter spreadsheet. It's taking the parts that are purely mechanical, working out who's due, sending the request, chasing a deadline, filing the evidence, out of a person's memory and into code that runs itself on a schedule. The part that isn't mechanical, deciding whether a vendor's answers are good enough, stays with a named human every time.

What it does

Every vendor in the register moves through the same cycle.

  1. Every weekday at 7am, the script checks the register against today's date to see who's due.
  2. A vendor whose reassessment falls within 30 days gets emailed the right questionnaire for its risk tier (full for Critical/High, lite for Medium/Low), with a 14-day deadline.
  3. No response by the deadline: a reminder goes out, then a final notice, then escalation to the business owner and security lead, repeating every 14 days until someone acts.
  4. When a vendor replies, running receive --vendor V001 --file response.xlsx safety-checks the file (refusing anything with macros, linked workbooks, or over 10MB), SHA-256 fingerprints it, and files it under evidence/V001/<date>/. Earlier files are never overwritten.
  5. The scoring engine reads the response by matching question labels rather than fixed cell coordinates, because vendors reformat sheets and label matching survives that. It checks 11 rules covering certification, pen testing, encryption, MFA, data location, access reviews, the security function, incidents, breach notification, business continuity and subprocessors.
  6. Encryption not in place, MFA not enforced, or Defence-related data held offshore are knockouts: any one of them forces a High rating regardless of the overall score, and the vendor cannot be approved without an explicit, recorded risk acceptance.
  7. A named human runs review to record the decision: approve, approve with conditions, reject, or request more information. Only an approval resets the vendor's reassessment clock.
  8. Every action, a request sent, a reminder, a reviewer's decision, is appended to a hash-chained audit log, so a later edit or deletion is detectable by running verify-log.

On Mondays, the security lead also gets a one-email digest of everything needing attention: escalated vendors, responses waiting too long for review, overdue reassessments and conditions, and vendors with no business owner on file.

A real run through the lifecycle

I ran the tool live (not a dry run) against the fictional register once, to see its own output rather than describe it secondhand. It sent 10 assessment requests and one repeat escalation as real .eml files, then I picked one vendor, SecureVault Offsite Backup, a newly onboarded High-tier vendor, and filled out its questionnaire myself to take it all the way through.

I answered mostly well, with one deliberate gap: MFA enforced for admin accounts but not yet rolled out to all staff. Running receive scored the response 70/100, but the engine found more than I'd planted:

SeverityFinding
CriticalMFA not enforced for staff with system access (knockout)
HighData stored offshore (United States, AWS us-east-1)
MediumPenetration test older than 12 months (435 days)
MediumOffshore subprocessor: ColdStore Infrastructure (Singapore)

The subprocessor finding wasn't something I'd set out to test; I'd added a second subprocessor row to the template almost as an afterthought, and the engine picked it up on its own.

The guardrail held before I'd even asked it to. I ran review --decision approve-with-conditions expecting it to go through. It refused outright: "This response has knockout findings: MFA not enforced for staff with system access. Approval requires --accept-risk."

This is the rule from the README working exactly as documented, not a hypothetical. A Critical finding doesn't lower a score, it blocks the approval path entirely until someone writes down why they're accepting the risk.

I re-ran it with an explicit --accept-risk justification naming the risk owner, and the decision went through: Approved with Conditions, next reassessment due in six months, with two conditions on file (enforce MFA within 30 days, provide a signed risk acceptance for the offshore storage). verify-log confirmed all 18 rows of the audit log still chain correctly, including the three new rows this run added.

Architecture

The design rule is the same one behind the GRC evidence-collection agent: the scoring engine can recommend, but it can never approve, and nothing counts as done until a named human says so.

Lessons from building and running it

  • The knockout rule has to be a hard stop, not a warning. When it refused my approval outright until I supplied a reason, that's what made the test worth doing. A rule a reviewer can click past under deadline pressure isn't a rule.
  • The scoring engine finds things you didn't plant. I set out to test the MFA knockout and the engine also caught a stale pen test and an offshore subprocessor I'd added almost as an afterthought. That's the value of checking every answer against every rule, rather than only the ones a reviewer remembers to look for.
  • Match by label, not coordinates. Vendors insert rows, merge cells and reformat sheets. Matching the question text in column A survives that; matching a fixed cell reference silently reads the wrong answer.
  • Only an approval should reset a compliance clock. It's tempting to mark a vendor "assessed" the moment a request goes out. That's the mistake an auditor catches immediately, so the status only moves forward on a recorded human decision.
  • A tamper-evident log is cheap to build and expensive to fake. Chaining a hash from one row to the next costs almost nothing to implement, but it turns "prove this log wasn't edited" from a trust exercise into something you can actually check.

What's next
